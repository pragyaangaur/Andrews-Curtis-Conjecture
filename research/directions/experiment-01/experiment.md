## 1. Summary

This experiment asks how far a fixed-effort Andrews-Curtis search gets on the Akbulut-Kirby presentations, using a checker that replays every move from the start. The search is a seeded random walk on pairs of relators, it accepts moves by a Metropolis rule on the total length, and it refuses every intermediate tuple whose total length exceeds a fixed cap. Two inputs with a known reduction, AK(2) and a length twelve Miller-Schupp presentation, are included as controls. The walk trivialises both controls in every seed and produces certificates that replay to the standard tuple. The walk does not lower the total length of AK(3), AK(4) or AK(5) in any seed, and a separate exhaustive check shows that no single Andrews-Curtis move shortens any of the five inputs.

## 2. Inputs, and a check that they present the trivial group

A word is stored as a tuple of nonzero signed integers, where the letter $a$ means $x_{|a|}$ for $a>0$ and $x_{|a|}^{-1}$ for $a<0$. Free reduction cancels every adjacent inverse pair. A balanced presentation on two generators is stored as a pair of reduced words.

The Akbulut-Kirby presentation is
$$\mathrm{AK}(n)=\langle x,y \mid xyx=yxy,\; x^n=y^{n+1}\rangle .$$
Its two relators, in the form used by the code, are $r_1=xyx(yxy)^{-1}=x\,y\,x\,y^{-1}x^{-1}y^{-1}$ and $r_2=x^n y^{-(n+1)}$. The lengths are 6 and $2n+1$, so the total length is $2n+7$, which is 11 for $n=2$, 13 for $n=3$, 15 for $n=4$ and 17 for $n=5$.

The Miller-Schupp input is the presentation quoted by Havas and Ramsay from Miller and Schupp [MS99]:
$$\mathrm{MS}(2)=\langle x,y \mid x^{-1}y^{2}x=y^{3},\; x=yxy^{-1}x^{-1}\rangle .$$
As a pair of relators this is $x^{-1}y^{2}xy^{-3}$ and $x^{2}yx^{-1}y^{-1}$, of lengths 7 and 5, so the total length is 12. Havas and Ramsay list four length twelve Miller-Schupp examples with $w=y^{\pm1}xy^{\pm1}x^{-1}$, and this is one of them.

Both families must present the trivial group, and this is an input to the experiment, so I checked it with GAP 4.14.0 coset enumeration. The script was
```gap
F := FreeGroup(2);; x := F.1;; y := F.2;;
for n in [1,2,3,4,5] do
  G := F/[x*y*x*(y*x*y)^-1, x^n*(y^(n+1))^-1];
  Print("AK(", n, ") order = ", Order(G), "\n");
od;
```
and the output was `AK(1) order = 1` through `AK(5) order = 1`. The same command on the four Miller-Schupp examples returned order 1 for each of them, with relator lengths 7 and 5. A coset enumeration is an exact computation, and the command above can be rerun by anyone with GAP installed, so this meets the swarm's standard for a computation.

One further check is cheap and it appears in the test suite. The exponent-sum matrix of AK$(n)$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ n&-(n+1)\end{pmatrix}$, whose determinant is $-1$. Hence the abelian invariants of AK$(n)$ give no obstruction, which is what one expects from a presentation of the trivial group.

## 3. Words, moves and certificates

The three Andrews-Curtis moves are implemented as follows, with indices counted from 0.

- `inv i` replaces $r_i$ by $r_i^{-1}$.
- `mul i j s side` replaces $r_i$ by $r_i r_j^{s}$ (`side` is `R`) or by $r_j^{s}r_i$ (`side` is `L`), with $i\neq j$ and $s\in\{+1,-1\}$.
- `conj i c` replaces $r_i$ by $c\,r_i\,c^{-1}$, where $c$ is any reduced word.

The move set is symmetric. Every move has an inverse of the same three kinds: inversion is its own inverse, the inverse of a multiplication is a multiplication by $r_j^{-s}$ on the same side, and the inverse of a conjugation is a conjugation by $c^{-1}$. The equivalence relation generated by these moves is therefore exactly Andrews-Curtis equivalence, and left multiplication is included because $r_i\mapsto r_jr_i$ is a composition of a conjugation with a right multiplication and is consequently still an Andrews-Curtis move. The function `check_move` rejects malformed moves, for example a multiplication with $i=j$ or a conjugator that is not reduced, and `replay` recomputes a state from the start tuple and checks every move on the way.

A certificate is a text file with one `start` line per relator and one line per move, as in
```
# start tuple
start 1 2 1 -2 -1 -2
start 1 1 -2 -2 -2
# moves
conj 0 1
mul 1 0 +1 R
inv 1
```
The standalone checker is `python3 ac_experiment.py verify --cert results/cert_AK2_seed1.txt`, and it re-derives the whole sequence with the same `replay` function that the tests exercise. The test suite also contains a second implementation of the three moves, written independently of `ac_experiment.py`, and it replays four of the shipped certificates with that second code path. The checker is the only thing that decides whether a reported reduction counts, and it never consults the search.

## 4. The gluing composite, and why it is needed

Conjugating a cyclically reduced word cannot make it shorter, because its length is already the minimum in its conjugacy class. Multiplying $r_i$ by $r_j^{s}$ changes the total length by $|r_j|-2c$, where $c$ is the number of letters of the overlap between the end of $r_i$ and the start of $r_j^{s}$. For all five inputs the largest such overlap is 1 (Section 6), and every relator has length at least 5, so every single multiplication increases the total length by at least 3 and no single move lowers it.

A reduction therefore has to start with a composite step. The step used here, called a gluing step, chooses a cyclic starting point in $r_i$ and in $r_j$, conjugates both relators so that those rotations are being read, right-multiplies $r_i$ by $r_j^{\pm1}$, and finally conjugates $r_i$ to cyclically reduce it. Each gluing step is three or four elementary moves, and `apply_gluing` returns the elementary list, so a gluing certificate is an ordinary Andrews-Curtis certificate. Working with rotations is legitimate because conjugation is a move and because every state in the search is kept cyclically reduced.

The gluing step is what the published Havas-Ramsay chain uses at every arrow, as Section 9 shows. It is also what makes the controls work. The same fixed step set solves AK(2) and the Miller-Schupp input, and it gets nowhere on AK(3) to AK(5) within the caps used here.

## 5. The search

A state is a pair of cyclically reduced words. A proposal picks a component $i$, the other component $j$, a sign $s$ and two cyclic starting points, then applies the gluing step. A proposal is rejected if it would create an empty relator or if the new total length would exceed the cap. Otherwise the proposal is accepted when the new total length is at most the current one, and it is accepted with probability $\exp(-d/T)$ when the new total length exceeds the current one by $d>0$. The temperature falls geometrically from $T_0$ to $T_1$ over the accepted move budget.

The effort is fixed in advance. A run stops after a given number of accepted gluing steps, and a hard cap on the number of proposals prevents a frozen local minimum from looping. The parameters used for the runs in Table 1 are $T_0=6$, $T_1=2$, budget 200000 accepted gluing steps for every seed, and the caps listed in Table 1. The cap-25 runs used $T_0=8$ and $T_1=2$. The generator is `random.Random(seed)` with the seed printed in the results, so every run is deterministic and can be repeated exactly.

The objective is the main heuristic choice. Total length is the simplest measure of how far a tuple is from the standard one, and it is the measure that the published searches also use. It is not the only possible objective, and Section 13 returns to this point.

## 6. Result A: no single Andrews-Curtis move shortens any of the five inputs

For each input, `single_move_analysis` tries every inversion, every multiplication by the other relator on both sides with both signs, and every conjugation by a reduced word of length at most 4. The count is 626 moves per input. The table gives the total length before and after the best single move, and the largest cancellation overlap $c$ over all ordered pairs and signs.

| input | relator lengths | total length | best single move | max overlap $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AK(2) | 6, 5 | 11 | 11 | 1 |
| AK(3) | 6, 7 | 13 | 13 | 1 |
| AK(4) | 6, 9 | 15 | 15 | 1 |
| AK(5) | 6, 11 | 17 | 17 | 1 |
| MS(2) | 7, 5 | 12 | 12 | 1 |

The computation agrees with an argument that covers conjugators of every length. Inversions preserve length. Conjugation cannot take a cyclically reduced word below its own length. A multiplication by $r_j^{s}$ changes the total length by $|r_j|-2c$ with $c\le1$, and since $|r_j|\ge5$ every multiplication increases the total length by at least 3. Hence each of the five inputs is a strict local minimum of total length under all single Andrews-Curtis moves, and a length-reducing walk has to pass through longer tuples. This is the precise sense in which these presentations are hard for local search, and it also explains why my first, purely elementary search never improved any of them.

## 7. Result B: exhaustive search to gluing depth two

The random walk is not exhaustive, so I also enumerated every tuple reachable from each input by at most two gluing steps, with every intermediate tuple under the cap. This is a deterministic finite check and it needs no seed.

| input | cap | tuples at depth 1 | tuples at depth 2 | all tuples up to depth 2 | least total length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AK(2) | 15 | 56 | 448 | 505 | 11 |
| AK(3) | 20 | 168 | 1329 | 1498 | 13 |
| MS(2) | 15 | 38 | 200 | 239 | 10 |

For AK(3) the least total length anywhere within two gluing steps of the input is 13, the value at the input itself. For AK(2) the value stays at 11, while for the Miller-Schupp input two gluing steps already reach a tuple of total length 10. The depth-two check shows that the controls and AK(3) behave differently at a distance where the check is exhaustive, and it does not say anything about greater depth.

The contrast between the controls and AK(3) is the main empirical finding. The same step set, the same acceptance rule and the same effort trivialise AK(2) and the Miller-Schupp input in every seed, with certificates between 184 and 1033 elementary moves long, and they leave AK(3) at 13 in every seed.

## 9. Result D: verification of the published AK(2) trivialization

Havas and Ramsay [HR03, Section 3] display a chain of 14 arrows that takes the length eleven presentation to $(b^{-1},a^{-1})$. In their notation upper case letters are inverses, and the chain is
```
(aBBBa, BAbabA) -> (aBBBa, BAbaBBa) -> (aBBBa, AbaBBaB) ->
(aBBaBBaB, AbaBBaB) -> (aBBaBBaB, bAbbABa) -> (aBBaBBaB, abAbbAB) ->
(aBBaBBaB, BabAbbA) -> (aBBaBBaB, BaBaB) -> (BBaBBaBa, BaBaB) ->
(BBaBBaBa, bAbAb) -> (BBaBBaBa, AbAbb) -> (BBa, AbAbb) -> (BBa, Ab) ->
(B, Ab) -> (B, A).
```
Each arrow is called an essential move in the paper. I checked each arrow separately. Seven of the arrows are gluing steps with both cyclic starting points at 0, and the other seven are cyclic rotations or inversions of one relator. The whole chain expands to 19 elementary moves, and the total length along it is
$$11 \to 12 \to 12 \to 15 \to 15 \to 15 \to 15 \to 13 \to 13 \to 13 \to 13 \to 8 \to 5 \to 3 \to 2 .$$
The chain ends at $(a^{-1},b^{-1})$, and two inversions turn that into $(a,b)$. The certificate is in `results/havas_ramsay_ak2.cert.txt`, and `python3 ac_experiment.py verify --cert results/havas_ramsay_ak2.cert.txt` replays it from AK(2) to the standard tuple.

Two details of the published chain need care and are handled explicitly in `verify_hr.py`. First, the paper's tuple is $(a^{2}b^{-3}, r_1^{-1})$ while my AK(2) is $(r_1, a^{2}b^{-3})$, so the two relators are swapped; the Andrews-Curtis move set is invariant under permuting the relator indices, so the permutation is applied to every index in the chain. Second, the second relator of the paper's first tuple is an inverse of mine, and the inversion is emitted as an explicit move. With those two adjustments, all 14 arrows verify.

The chain shows that this 14-arrow proof passes through total length 15, since the total length rises from 11 to 15 before falling to 2. It also shows that a search for AK(2) must be allowed to grow the words before it can succeed, which is why the cap for the AK(2) control was set to 15.

## 10. Result E: a deterministic Miller-Schupp certificate

The Miller-Schupp input does not need the random walk. A deterministic best-improvement descent over all gluing steps, with no randomness at all, reduces it from total length 12 to the standard tuple in 19 elementary moves. The descent tries every pair of components, both signs and every pair of cyclic starting points, and it takes the move that gives the least total length, repeating until no move improves. The trajectory of total lengths is
$$12 \to 12 \to 17 \to 11 \to 11 \to 11 \to 16 \to 10 \to 10 \to 13 \to 9 \to 13 \to 7 \to 7 \to 6 \to 4 \to 5 \to 3 \to 4 \to 2 .$$
The certificate is `results/ms2_polish.cert.txt`, and it replays to $(x,y)$ with no extra moves. This is an independent certificate of Andrews-Curtis triviality for one presentation of the Miller-Schupp family, found without a seeded search and checkable by the same replay function.

The same deterministic descent does nothing to AK(2), AK(3), AK(4) or AK(5), since no single gluing step lowers their total length. The descent is therefore a cheap test that separates the Miller-Schupp input from the Akbulut-Kirby inputs at depth one.

## 12. Interpretation

This section is interpretation and contains no new computation.

The step set and the checker are good enough to solve the controls. A 200000-step walk with a total-length cap of 15 finds a certificate from AK(2) to the standard tuple in every one of three seeds, with certificates of 225, 499 and 394 elementary moves. The same walk finds Miller-Schupp certificates in every seed, and a deterministic descent finds one in 19 moves. This matters because it shows that a failure on AK(3) is not an artefact of a broken move generator or of a checker that accepts nothing: the same code accepts and verifies thousands of moves on the controls.

AK(3) is a strict local minimum of total length, so no local search can reduce it by one move. The walk must first make the tuple longer, and the caps used here allow it to explore tuples up to total length 20 or 25. Within those caps the walk visited about 170000 distinct tuples per seed at cap 20, and the two cap-25 runs visited about 195000 each, without ever producing a tuple below 13. The depth-two exhaustive check agrees: within two gluing steps and under the cap, the least total length reachable from AK(3) is 13.

The literature points the same way. Havas and Ramsay [HR03] prove that a balanced two-generator presentation of the trivial group with total relator length at most 13 is Andrews-Curtis equivalent to the standard presentation or to AK(3), so AK(3) is the unique smallest potential counterexample in that family. Panteleev and Ushakov [PU19] enumerate presentations Andrews-Curtis equivalent to AK(3) whose relators all have length at most 20, and they report that the enumeration found no standard presentation and that it needed 207 CPU days. My cap bounds the total length rather than each relator, so my search covers a different and smaller region than theirs, and my result is a sample from that region rather than an enumeration of it.

Nothing here bears on whether AK(3) is Andrews-Curtis trivial. A heuristic that fails is not evidence of non-equivalence, and the only exhaustive statements in this file are the single-move check of Section 6 and the depth-two check of Section 7. The honest reading is that under this objective the AK(3) gap is much wider than the AK(2) gap, and that the AK(2) certificate is a warning about length-guided search near length 13.

## 13. Limitations and failure modes

The random walk is not exhaustive. It reports what a fixed number of accepted moves found, and a different move set, objective or temperature schedule could find more. The depth-two search in Section 7 is exhaustive, but it says nothing about depth three and beyond.

The cap and the objective interact. A total-length cap of 20 excludes tuples whose individual relators are long even when the total is short, and Panteleev and Ushakov cap each relator instead. The two constraints are not comparable, so the two experiments do not test the same region.

The search does not quotient by automorphisms of the free group. Panteleev and Ushakov use such a quotient to shrink the space, and my distinct-state counts therefore overcount tuples that are related by a symmetry the walk does not exploit.

The certificates are only as trustworthy as the replay checker. The checker is short, it has its own tests, and every certificate reported here was replayed with it from the start tuple; the test suite additionally replays four of the certificates through a second implementation that shares no code with the first. The triviality of the inputs was checked with GAP coset enumeration, which is exact but is not a formal proof.

Finally, the experiment does not attempt AK(3) beyond the cap of 25, and it does not attempt the Miller-Schupp presentations with larger parameters. The published record for AK(3) is a search over relator length at most 20 and a much larger computational budget, and this experiment is not competitive with that record. It is a small and reproducible data point, and it is checked independently.
