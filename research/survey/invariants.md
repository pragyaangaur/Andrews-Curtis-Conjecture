# Invariants for Andrews-Curtis equivalence, and why the classical ones cannot separate

This file is the Round 1 deliverable for direction D1. It maps the invariants that the
literature proposes for Andrews-Curtis (AC) equivalence. For each one it states exactly what
the three moves do to it, and exactly where that invariant fails to separate the standard
presentation from the standard suspects AK(3) and the Miller-Schupp family. It then explains
why finite quotients cannot help, and what a separating invariant would have to be.

The labels used throughout this file are the following, and every claim carries one of
them. Each label marks how the claim was checked.

- **Argument** means a proof written out here and checked by hand, not by Lean.
- **Computation** means an exact, seeded, rerunnable program. The programs are in
  [survey/invariants_checks/](invariants_checks/) and the exact commands are in Section 12.
- **Cited** means a statement taken from a source listed in Section 13, with the source
  identified. I did not reprove these statements.

Nothing here claims a proof or a disproof of the conjecture. The results are a no-go
statement for a large family of invariants, a table of the values those invariants take on
the standard test presentations, and one precise description of what a working invariant
would have to be.

## 1. Setting and notation

Let $F_n$ be the free group on $x_1,\dots,x_n$. A *presentation* in this file is a tuple
$P=(r_1,\dots,r_n)$ of $n$ words in $F_n$; it is *balanced* because the number of relators
equals the number of generators. Write $N(P)$ for the normal closure of $\{r_1,\dots,r_n\}$
in $F_n$ and $G(P)=F_n/N(P)$ for the group presented. Write
$\pi_P\colon F_n \twoheadrightarrow G(P)$ for the quotient map, with its distinguished
generating tuple $(\pi_P(x_1),\dots,\pi_P(x_n))$.

The three AC moves on the tuple are:

- (A1) $r_i \mapsto r_i^{-1}$;
- (A2) $r_i \mapsto r_i r_j$ for $i\neq j$;
- (A3) $r_i \mapsto w r_i w^{-1}$ for an arbitrary word $w\in F_n$.

AC-equivalence is the equivalence relation generated by these moves. The Andrews-Curtis
conjecture (**[AC65]**) asserts that every balanced presentation of the trivial group is
AC-equivalent to the standard tuple $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$. In the literature the moves are the
*Q-transformations*, and Hog-Angeloni and Metzler record them in their list
(26). That list contains inversion of a relator, conjugation of a relator, and multiplication
of one relator by another on the right or on the left, and in each case the relators with the
other indices remain unchanged (**[HA-M93a]**). Left multiplication is a composite of the
three moves, since $r_j r_i$ is obtained from $r_i$ by replacing $r_i$ with $r_i r_j$ and
then conjugating by $r_j$. So the two conventions agree. The *stable* version adds the
prolongation move that introduces a new generator together with that generator as a relator,
and its inverse;
**[Bar25]** lists all six moves as (i) to (vi). The stable version is the subject of
direction D10 and appears here only where an invariant also fails stably.

## 2. The one thing the moves preserve exactly

**Lemma 1 (Argument).** Let $P'$ be obtained from $P$ by one AC move. Then
$N(P')=N(P)$ as subgroups of $F_n$.

*Proof.* For (A1), $r_i$ and $r_i^{-1}$ generate the same normal subgroup. For (A2),
$r_i=(r_i r_j)r_j^{-1}$ lies in the normal closure of $\{r_i r_j, r_j\}$, and $r_i r_j$ lies
in the normal closure of $\{r_i,r_j\}$, so the two normal closures coincide. For (A3),
$w r_i w^{-1}$ lies in the normal closure of $r_i$, and $r_i = w^{-1}(w r_i w^{-1})w$ lies
in the normal closure of $w r_i w^{-1}$. $\square$

The computation file checks the three certificates of this proof on 300 seeded random
tuples in $F_3$: $r_i=(r_i r_j)r_j^{-1}$ holds as an identity of reduced words, and
conjugation and inversion are invertible on reduced words. This is a spot check of the word
identities, and the general statement is the argument above.

**Corollary 2.** Every AC move leaves the quotient map $\pi_P\colon F_n\twoheadrightarrow G(P)$
unchanged. The source, its basis, the kernel and the target are the same before and after
the move.

**Corollary 3 (a no-go statement for a large family of invariants).** Let $I$ be any
function of presentations that factors through $\pi_P$, that is, $I(P)$ depends only on
$(F_n, x_1,\dots,x_n, N(P))$. Then $I$ is constant on AC classes. In particular, if $P$
and $P'$ are balanced presentations of the trivial group, then $\pi_P$ and $\pi_{P'}$ are
both the map $F_n\twoheadrightarrow 1$, so $I(P)=I(P')$.

Corollary 3 is the structural reason for everything in Sections 3 to 7. Every invariant in
those sections is a function of $N(P)$ alone, sometimes after applying a functor. The
common value that these invariants take on the whole class of balanced presentations of the
trivial group is computed in the next sections, and it is always the value attached to the
trivial group.

A useful way to say the same thing is this. The map $P\mapsto N(P)$ is an AC-invariant, so
AC classes are contained in the fibres of that map. The fibre over $F_n$ consists exactly
of the presentations of the trivial group. The conjecture asserts that this fibre is a
single AC class at each rank $n$. Any invariant that factors through $N$ is constant on the
fibre by definition, so it cannot see the difference the conjecture is about.

## 3. The exponent-sum matrix

For a word $w$ and a letter $j$, let $\varepsilon_j(w)\in\mathbb{Z}$ be the exponent sum of
$x_j$ in $w$. For a presentation $P$ let $M(P)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}$ be the matrix whose
$i$-th row is $(\varepsilon_1(r_i),\dots,\varepsilon_n(r_i))$. This is the matrix of the
abelianised relation module, also called the exponent-sum matrix.

**Lemma 4 (Argument, checked by computation).** For each move there is a unimodular
integer matrix $U\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ with $M(P')=U\,M(P)$:

- (A1) at index $i$: $U$ is the identity with the $i$-th diagonal entry replaced by $-1$;
- (A2) at indices $i,j$: $U$ is the identity plus the matrix unit $E_{ij}$;
- (A3): $U$ is the identity, because conjugation preserves exponent sums.

*Proof.* The $i$-th row of $M$ is the image of $r_i$ under the abelianisation map
$F_n\to\mathbb{Z}^n$. Abelianisation is a homomorphism, so the row is additive under (A2)
and changes sign under (A1), and it is invariant under conjugation. $\square$

The computation applies 7200 seeded random moves at ranks 2, 3 and 4 and confirms
$M(P')=U\,M(P)$ exactly, with the predicted $U$. The repetitions at three different ranks
check that the pattern of the matrices $U$ does not depend on the rank.

**Corollary.** The orbit $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})\cdot M(P)$ is an AC-invariant. So are its
orbit invariants: $|\det M|$, the determinantal divisors (the gcd of all $k\times k$ minors),
the Smith normal form, the rank modulo a prime $p$, the elementary ideals of the matrix, and
the cokernel $\mathbb{Z}^n/\mathrm{rowspan}(M)$, which is $G(P)^{\mathrm{ab}}$.

**Proposition 5 (Argument).** If $G(P)=1$, then $M(P)$ is unimodular, that is,
$|\det M(P)|=1$ and the Smith normal form of $M(P)$ is the identity matrix $I_n$.

*Proof.* If $G(P)=1$ then $G(P)^{\mathrm{ab}}=0$, so $\mathbb{Z}^n/\mathrm{rowspan}(M)=0$.
Since $M$ is square, $\mathrm{rowspan}(M)=\mathbb{Z}^n$ means that $M$ has a left inverse
over $\mathbb{Z}$, hence $\det M=\pm 1$. The Smith normal form of a unimodular matrix is
$I_n$. $\square$

Proposition 5 is a real theorem with content, and it is worth seeing what the content is.
The invariant certifies that a presentation meeting the conjecture's hypothesis has
unimodular exponent-sum matrix, so a presentation of the trivial group whose exponent-sum
matrix is e.g. $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ cannot exist. On the
other hand, the proposition says that on the entire class of balanced presentations of the
trivial group the invariant has exactly one value. The computation confirms the value on a
battery of twelve presentations.

The table is computed exactly. The family AK(n) is the one introduced by Akbulut and Kirby
(**[AK85]**), and its entries use $r_1=xyx(yxy)^{-1}$ and $r_2=x^ny^{-(n+1)}$; the
Miller-Schupp entries use
$a^{-1}b^na\,b^{-(n+1)}$ and $aw^{-1}$, which is the family of **[MS99]**, Corollary 3.
Triviality of each group in the table is not assumed: it is checked independently by coset
enumeration in Section 12 for every row. The length-14 and length-27 rows are the first and
third entries in the list of known two-generator examples collected in **[MMS02]**, and
**[BM93]** is a survey of balanced presentations of the trivial group. The computations also
confirm that the Smith normal form of $M$ reproduces the abelian invariant factors on four
presentations whose abelian groups are known: $\langle x,y\mid x^2,y^2\rangle$ gives $(2,2)$,
$\langle x,y\mid x^2,y^3\rangle$ gives $(1,6)$, $\langle x,y\mid x^2y^{-2},y^4\rangle$
gives $(2,4)$ and $\langle x,y\mid x^3,y^3\rangle$ gives $(3,3)$.

The same invariant does have teeth outside the hypothesis of the conjecture. The
presentation $\langle x,y\mid x^2,y^2\rangle$ has $|\det M|=4$ and presents
$\mathbb{Z}/2*\mathbb{Z}/2$, and $\langle x,y\mid x^2,y^3\rangle$ has $|\det M|=6$ and
presents $\mathbb{Z}/2*\mathbb{Z}/3$. Neither can be AC-equivalent to the standard
presentation. So the exponent-sum matrix is a genuine obstruction whenever the
abelianisation is nontrivial. Since the hypothesis of the conjecture forces the
abelianisation to be trivial, the obstruction is vacuous exactly on the class in question.

The same collapse happens modulo every prime. If $G(P)=1$ then $M$ has rank $n$ modulo
every prime, so the mod-$p$ relation matrix is surjective and its cokernel vanishes. The
computation checks ranks $n$ modulo $2,3,5,7$ on all twelve presentations.

## 4. The relation module and Fox calculus

Let $G=G(P)$ and let $\mathbb{Z}G$ be the integral group ring. The *relation module* is
$N(P)_{\mathrm{ab}} = N(P)/[N(P),N(P)]$, made into a left $\mathbb{Z}G$-module by
conjugation through $F_n\twoheadrightarrow G$; see **[Joh97]**, Chapter 11, where the
relation module is defined and identified with the kernel of $\mathbb{Z}G^n\to I_G$, with
$I_G$ the augmentation ideal.

**Lemma 6 (Argument).** An AC move leaves the $\mathbb{Z}G$-module $N(P)_{\mathrm{ab}}$
unchanged, with the same generators.

*Proof.* By Lemma 1 the subgroup $N(P)$ is unchanged, and by Corollary 2 the map
$F_n\to G$ is unchanged. The abelianisation of a fixed subgroup, with the action induced by
a fixed map, is a fixed module. $\square$

Fox calculus makes the module computable from the words. For $j=1,\dots,n$ let
$\partial/\partial x_j\colon \mathbb{Z}F_n\to\mathbb{Z}F_n$ be the Fox derivative
(**[Fox53]**), and for a relator $r$ write
$\nabla(r)=(\partial r/\partial x_1,\dots,\partial r/\partial x_n)\in(\mathbb{Z}F_n)^n$.
Pushing forward along $F_n\to G$ gives $\mathbb{Z}G^n$. The standard exact sequence of
**[Joh97]**, Chapter 11, identifies $N(P)_{\mathrm{ab}}$ with the $\mathbb{Z}G$-submodule of
$\mathbb{Z}G^n$ generated by the vectors $\nabla(r_i)$. Since $N(P)$ is fixed by the moves,
so is that submodule, and the dependence on the particular relator words disappears.

**Proposition 7 (Argument, checked by computation).** If $G(P)=1$ then:

- $\mathbb{Z}G=\mathbb{Z}$, and $N(P)_{\mathrm{ab}}=F_n^{\mathrm{ab}}=\mathbb{Z}^n$;
- the Fox matrix at the trivial representation, whose $(i,j)$ entry is the evaluation of
  $\partial r_i/\partial x_j$ at $x_k\mapsto 1$, is exactly $M(P)$;
- the submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ generated by the Fox vectors is all of $\mathbb{Z}^n$,
  and the zeroth elementary ideal $E_0$ is the unit ideal.

*Proof.* The first item holds because $N(P)=F_n$. For the second, evaluating
$\partial/\partial x_j$ at $x_k\mapsto 1$ gives the exponent sum in $x_j$, by the defining
properties of the Fox derivative. The third follows from Proposition 5. $\square$

The computation checks the second item on all twelve presentations by evaluating the Fox
derivatives symbolically and comparing with $M$. The comparison is an exact identity of
integer polynomials.

The refinement that carries information in general is the class of the Fox matrix modulo
left multiplication by $\mathrm{GE}_n(\mathbb{Z}G)$, the group generated by elementary and
diagonal matrices. That quotient is where the only known working invariants of this kind
live; Section 9 describes them. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the refinement collapses, and this is the
precise point of failure. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is Euclidean, so the Euclidean algorithm
reduces any matrix to Smith form by elementary row and column operations and sign changes.
Hence $\mathrm{GE}_n(\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ for every $n$, and the class of
$M$ modulo $\mathrm{GE}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ is its ordinary left orbit, which by Proposition 5
contains $I_n$ for every presentation of the trivial group.

## 5. Alexander modules, mod-$p$ covers and twisted invariants

An Alexander module needs a homomorphism $G(P)\to\mathbb{Z}$. If $G(P)=1$ the only such
homomorphism is trivial, the infinite cyclic cover is the complex itself, and the Alexander
module is $H_1$ of that complex, which vanishes because the complex is contractible
(Section 7). In Fox terms, the Alexander matrix at the trivial map is the Fox matrix
evaluated at $x_k\mapsto 1$, which is $M(P)$ by Proposition 7, and its entries have gcd $1$
for every presentation of the trivial group. So the Alexander polynomial is $1$ up to units
and the Alexander module is $0$.

I checked that the Fox implementation is not vacuous by using it on groups where the
Alexander polynomial is known. For the trefoil group $\langle x,y\mid x^2=y^3\rangle$ it
returns $t^2-t+1$, and for the torus knot group $\langle x,y\mid x^2=y^5\rangle$ it returns
$t^4-t^3+t^2-t+1$. Both are the standard values of the Alexander polynomial of the torus
knots $T(2,3)$ and $T(2,5)$, computed from the abelianisation $x\mapsto t^3$, $y\mapsto t^2$
respectively $x\mapsto t^5$, $y\mapsto t^2$. So the code computes the classical invariant
correctly on cases where it is nonzero, and returns the trivial value on the class of
interest.

The mod-$p$ covering invariants have the same fate. Fix a prime $p$ and a representation
$\rho\colon G(P)\to\mathrm{GL}_k(\mathbb{F}_p)$. The twisted relation matrix $A(\rho)$ has
$(i,j)$ entry $\rho(\partial r_i/\partial x_j)$, and the first homology of the corresponding
covering with twisted coefficients is the cokernel of $A(\rho)$. If $G(P)=1$ then the only
$\rho$ is trivial, $A(\rho)$ is the reduction of $M(P)$ modulo $p$, and the cokernel
vanishes because $M(P)$ has rank $n$ modulo $p$. The computation checks the rank for
$p=2,3,5,7$ on the whole battery.

There is a general statement behind these computations. Every invariant built by composing
Fox derivatives with a representation of $G(P)$ factors through the map $\pi_P$ of
Corollary 2, once the relator tuple is replaced by the submodule it generates. Corollary 3
then applies.

## 6. Why finite quotients cannot help

**Proposition 8 (Argument).** Let $P$ and $P'$ be balanced presentations of the trivial
group. For every finite group $Q$, the set of homomorphisms $F_n\to Q$ that kill all
relators of $P$ equals the set of homomorphisms that kill all relators of $P'$. Both sets
consist of the trivial homomorphism alone. Consequently $P$ and $P'$ have the same finite
quotients, the same profinite completion, and the same sets of maps to every finite group.

*Proof.* A homomorphism $F_n\to Q$ kills the relators of $P$ exactly when it factors through
$G(P)=1$. The only such homomorphism is trivial. The same holds for $P'$. $\square$

The tempting mistake is to look for a finite quotient in which the relator *words* behave
differently. The words do differ along an AC path: (A2) replaces $r_i$ by $r_i r_j$ and
(A3) replaces $r_i$ by $w r_i w^{-1}$, so the images of the individual relators in a fixed
finite group change. What does not change is the set of maps that kill the whole tuple,
because that set is governed by the normal closure, and the normal closure is fixed by
Lemma 1. An invariant built from the images of the individual relators is usually destroyed
by a single move, and so it is not an AC-invariant. An invariant built from the maps that
kill the tuple is an invariant of $N(P)$ and is therefore constant by Corollary 3. There is
no third option inside this family.

The computation confirms Proposition 8 on six finite groups. For $Q=C_2,C_6,S_3,S_4,A_5,D_8$
it enumerates all $|Q|^2$ homomorphisms $F_2\to Q$ and counts those that kill the relators.
For the standard presentation and for AK(3) the count is $1$ in every case, and the same
holds for a Miller-Schupp presentation.

| $Q$ | pairs tested | killing the standard relators | killing the AK(3) relators |
|---|---|---|---|
| $C_2$ | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| $C_6$ | 36 | 1 | 1 |
| $S_3$ | 36 | 1 | 1 |
| $S_4$ | 576 | 1 | 1 |
| $A_5$ | 3600 | 1 | 1 |
| $D_8$ | 64 | 1 | 1 |

## 7. The presentation complex

Associate to $P$ the standard 2-complex $K_P$ with one 0-cell, one 1-cell for each
generator and one 2-cell for each relator. Its Euler characteristic is $1-n+n=1$, its
fundamental group is $G(P)$, and its cellular chain complex in dimensions 2 and 1 is
$\mathbb{Z}^n\xrightarrow{M(P)}\mathbb{Z}^n$.

**Proposition 9 (Argument).** If $G(P)=1$ then $K_P$ is contractible.

*Proof.* By Proposition 5, $M(P)$ is unimodular, so $\partial_2$ is injective and
$\partial_1$ is surjective. Hence $H_0(K_P)=\mathbb{Z}$ and $H_1=H_2=0$. Since $K_P$ is
simply connected, the Hurewicz theorem gives $\pi_2=H_2=0$ and then $\pi_k=0$ for all $k$.
A CW complex with vanishing homotopy groups is contractible by Whitehead's theorem. $\square$

So every balanced presentation of the trivial group has a contractible standard 2-complex.
All homotopy invariants of $K_P$ are those of a point, and no homotopy invariant can
separate two such presentations. The Whitehead group of the trivial group is
$\mathrm{Wh}(1)=0$ (**[BHS64]**), so every homotopy equivalence between such complexes is
simple and the simple homotopy type carries no additional information either. Whitehead
torsion is identically zero here, and so is Reidemeister torsion.

What does carry information is the 3-deformation class. Hog-Angeloni and Metzler prove that
3-deformation types of compact connected 2-complexes correspond bijectively to
$Q^{**}$-classes of finite presentations (**[HA-M93a]**, Theorem 2.4). Barmak states that
the weak form of the conjecture, which allows $Q^*$-transformations, is equivalent to the
statement that every contractible finite 2-dimensional CW complex 3-deforms to a point
(**[Bar25]**, introduction). The generalized form of the conjecture for simple homotopy
equivalent complexes is discussed in **[HA-M93b]**. The unstable conjecture is a statement
about a purely combinatorial move on 2-dimensional complexes, and the topological formulation
adds no
homotopy-theoretic invariant that could be computed instead. The topology survey of
direction D6 covers the 3-deformation machinery and Zeeman's conjecture.

## 8. Why the third move defeats Nielsen and Whitehead invariants

It is natural to try Nielsen equivalence of the relator tuple, since Nielsen equivalence is
a classical invariant of generating tuples, and Whitehead's algorithm decides the
corresponding automorphism problem in the free group. This route fails, and the reason is
worth recording exactly.

The move (A2) is not the effect of an automorphism of $F_n$ on the tuple. Applying the
automorphism $\varphi$ with $\varphi(x_i)=x_i x_j$ to the tuple replaces $r_i$ by
$r_i(x_1,\dots,x_i x_j,\dots,x_n)$, which for a general word $r_i$ is not $r_i r_j$; for
$r_i=x_i^2$ it is $(x_i x_j)^2$. So (A2) is a row operation on the tuple, and not a change
of basis of $F_n$.

Worse, the subgroup generated by the relators is destroyed by (A3). The first two moves do
preserve it, since $\{r_i r_j,r_j\}$ and $\{r_i,r_j\}$ generate the same subgroup and
inverting a generator does not change a subgroup. Conjugation does not preserve it. Take the
standard tuple $(x,y)$ in $F_2$ and conjugate $x$ by $w=xy$. The result is
$(x y x y^{-1}x^{-1},\,y)$, and the subgroup $S=\langle y,\, xyx y^{-1}x^{-1}\rangle$ is
proper. To see this, map $F_2$ onto the infinite dihedral group acting on $\mathbb{Z}$ by
$p(n)=-n$ and $q(n)=1-n$, with $y\mapsto p$ and $x\mapsto q$. The relator $xyxy^{-1}x^{-1}$
maps to $qpqpq$, and $(qpqpq)(n)=3-n$ for every $n$. Write $t(n)=n+1$, so that $q=tp$ and
the image of $F_2$ is the full isometry group $\langle p,t\rangle$ of $\mathbb{Z}$. Every
element of $\langle p,\,n\mapsto 3-n\rangle$ has the form $n\mapsto \pm n+3k$, and that
set is a subgroup of index $3$ in $\langle p,t\rangle$. The translation $t$ is not in it,
because $t(0)=1$ is not a multiple of $3$. So the image of $S$ is a proper subgroup of the
image of $F_2$, and $S$ is a proper subgroup of $F_2$. The computation checks the affine
composition $(qpqpq)(n)=3-n$ and the closure of $\{n\mapsto \pm n+3k\}$ under composition.
The moved tuple has exponent-sum matrix $I_2$, so the example is consistent with
Proposition 5 and the obstruction there is not what is being used.

Consequently a computation showing that the relators of a tuple generate a proper subgroup
of $F_n$ cannot obstruct AC-triviality, because a single conjugation can change the subgroup.
This is the exact point where the Nielsen and Whitehead route fails, and it fails for every
presentation and not only for the standard suspects. For AK(3) the abelianised picture gives
no obstruction of this kind either: the exponent-sum vectors of the two relators are $(1,-1)$
and $(3,-4)$, and their determinant is $-1$, so they already span $\mathbb{Z}^2$. Whether
those two words generate $F_2$ as a subgroup is a separate question, which I did not settle
and which the argument above shows to be irrelevant to AC-triviality.

What survives from this circle of ideas is only $N(P)$ itself, together with its orbit under
$\mathrm{Aut}(F_n)$. For presentations of the trivial group, $N(P)=F_n$ and that orbit is a
single point. The AC moves do not even apply automorphisms of $F_n$ to the relators, so no
Nielsen data survives at all.

## 9. Invariants that do have content, and why they do not reach the trivial-group class

The strongest published invariant of this kind is Barmak's winding invariant
(**[Bar19]**, **[Bar25]**). For a word $w$ in the commutator subgroup of $F_2$, the winding
invariant is a Laurent polynomial $P_w\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm1},Y^{\pm1}]$, obtained from the
winding numbers of a closed curve in the grid $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}\cup
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}$ associated with $w$. A presentation is called *cocommutative*
when all its relators lie in $[F_2,F_2]$, and for a cocommutative presentation with $m$
relators one forms the vector $\Lambda(P)=(P_{r_1},\dots,P_{r_m})\in R^m$ with
$R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm1},Y^{\pm1}]$. A $Q$-transformation changes $\Lambda$ by adding one
entry to another, by a sign, or by multiplying an entry by a monomial $X^kY^l$, so
$\Lambda(Q)^t=E\,\Lambda(P)^t$ for some $E\in\mathrm{GE}_m(R)$ whenever $Q$ is
$Q$-equivalent to $P$ (**[Bar25]**, Lemma 3 and the paragraph following it).

This invariant has content because $R$ is not a generalized Euclidean ring. The title of
**[BM82]** records the result as $E_2\neq\mathrm{SL}_2$ for most Laurent polynomial rings,
and the review summary of **[Ev99]** states it as $\mathrm{SL}_2(R_m)\neq E_2(R_m)$ for
$m\geq 2$, with $R_m$ the Laurent polynomial ring in $m$ variables. Barmak records the
consequence as $\mathrm{GE}_n(R)\neq\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ for
$R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm1},Y^{\pm1}]$, and he states that Evans gave an explicit $2\times 2$
matrix over $R$ that is invertible and is not a product of elementary and diagonal matrices
(**[Ev99]**, Theorem C, as stated in Theorem 1 of **[Bar25]**). The two secondary sources
differ in the letter $E_2$ against $\mathrm{GE}_2$, so the exact statement should be read in
Evans' paper, which I did not obtain. I checked by computation that the displayed matrix has
determinant $1$ over $R$, so its membership in $\mathrm{GL}_2$ is confirmed. Barmak uses that
matrix and the winding invariant to construct presentations whose standard complexes are
simple homotopy equivalent but which are not $Q^*$-equivalent (**[Bar18]**, **[Bar25]**), and
that use relies on the $\mathrm{GE}_2$ form.

This line of work does not reach AK(3), for two separate reasons. First, the published
invariant is defined for cocommutative presentations, and AK(3) is not cocommutative: its
first relator $xyx(yxy)^{-1}$ has exponent sums $(1,-1)$. Second, the mechanism collapses
when the group is trivial. The general form of the winding invariant is described by Barmak
as the abelianisation map $N\to N/N'$ associated with a presentation, and for a presentation
of the trivial group that map is the identity $\mathbb{Z}^n\to\mathbb{Z}^n$; the coefficient
ring becomes $\mathbb{Z}$, which is Euclidean, so $\mathrm{GE}_n=\mathrm{GL}_n$ and the
matrix class is the Smith normal form by Proposition 5. Both reasons point at the same
structural fact: the invariant needs a nontrivial group ring to have room to be nonzero.

The literature also contains an unpublished manuscript by Browning, "The effect of
Curtis-Andrews moves on Jacobian matrices of perfect groups" (Cornell, 1976), cited by
Barmak. I could not obtain it and I make no claim about its content.

## 10. What a separating invariant would have to be

The survey up to here gives a precise description of the gap. Let $n$ be fixed and let
$\mathcal{T}_n$ be the set of $n$-tuples $P$ with $N(P)=F_n$. The conjecture says that
$\mathcal{T}_n$ is a single AC class, namely the class of $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$. By Lemma 1 the
map $P\mapsto N(P)$ is constant on AC classes, and on $\mathcal{T}_n$ it is constant with
value $F_n$. A separating invariant must therefore be a function of the relator tuple that

- is unchanged by (A1), by (A2) for every ordered pair of distinct indices, and by (A3) for
  every word $w$;
- does not factor through $N(P)$, since every function of $N(P)$ is constant on
  $\mathcal{T}_n$.

A natural question is whether $P\mapsto N(P)$ is the only obstruction in practice, in the
sense that two presentations with the same normal closure are always AC-equivalent. They are
not. Barmak reports an example of Metzler: the presentations
$\langle x,y\mid x^5,y^5,[x,y]\rangle$ and $\langle x,y\mid x^5,y^5,[x^2,y]\rangle$ have the
same normal closure and are $Q^*$-equivalent, and they are not $Q$-equivalent (**[Bar25]**,
citing Metzler). I checked by coset enumeration that both have order $25$; since $x^5=y^5=1$
in both, both present $\mathbb{Z}/5\times\mathbb{Z}/5$. So the fibres of $N$ are strictly
larger than AC classes already for a nontrivial group, and the conjecture says that the two
relations coincide on the fibre over $F_n$.

In the language of Section 7, the invariant must distinguish contractible 2-complexes up to
3-deformation. Homotopy invariants cannot do this, because all these complexes are
contractible. The invariant must be sensitive to the attaching words and insensitive to the
three moves, which is an unusual combination: (A2) destroys the individual relators and
(A3) destroys the subgroup they generate, so the only structure of the tuple that survives
is its normal closure, and that is constant on the class of interest.

Two remarks make this less hopeless than it sounds. The first is that the moves do not
destroy the *complex* up to 3-deformation, which is a 3-dimensional object even when the
2-complex is 2-dimensional; invariants of the thickening that are preserved by elementary
expansions and collapses are not excluded by anything in this file. The second is that an
AC-invariant need not be defined for all presentations. An invariant defined only on
cocommutative presentations, or only on presentations with a fixed abelianisation, can still
separate the pairs it is defined on, and Barmak's invariant is an example. What Section 9
shows is that the known examples of this kind need a nontrivial coefficient ring.

## 12. Reproduction

The programs are in [survey/invariants_checks/](invariants_checks/). They need python3 and
sympy, both present in the VM. Each run takes a few seconds.

```
cd survey/invariants_checks
python3 invariants_check.py      # 15 PASS lines, ends "ALL 15 CHECKS PASSED"
python3 triviality_check.py      # 17 PASS lines, ends "ALL TODD-COXETER CHECKS PASSED"
```

`invariants_check.py` is seeded with `random.Random(20261104)`, `Random(20261105)`,
`Random(20261106)`, `Random(20261108)` and `Random(7)` in its randomised parts, so the runs
are deterministic. Its checks are: free-group reduction and exponent-sum
identities; the action of the moves on $M$ (7200 move applications); invariance of the Smith
normal form and $|\det M|$ under random move sequences; the value table of Section 3; the
Smith form against known abelian invariants; the Fox matrix at the trivial representation;
the Alexander polynomials of two torus knot groups; the normal-closure certificates of
Lemma 1; the determinant of Evans' matrix; the finite-quotient counts of Section 6; and the
dihedral-group witness of Section 8.

`triviality_check.py` is an independent Todd-Coxeter coset enumeration over the trivial
subgroup. It reproduces the index correctly on three control presentations
($\langle x\mid x^5\rangle$ gives $5$, $\langle x\mid x^5,x^3\rangle$ gives $1$, and
$\langle x,y\mid x^2,y^3,(xy)^5\rangle$ gives $60$, the alternating group $A_5$), and then
returns index $1$ for AK(1) to AK(6), for the three Miller-Schupp presentations of the
table, for the length-14 presentation and for the Wicks length-27 presentation. It also
returns index $25$ for the two presentations in Metzler's pair. An index of
$1$ for the trivial subgroup is a proof that the group is trivial, because every
Todd-Coxeter deduction is valid in every quotient. The program reports "NO RESULT" and not
a failure if it hits its coset or step limit, so a limit cannot be mistaken for a proof.

The key output lines are recorded in `invariants_check.out` and `triviality_check.out` next
to the programs, and both output files are part of the deliverable. The commands above
regenerate them.
