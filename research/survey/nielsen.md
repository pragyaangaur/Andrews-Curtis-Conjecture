# Nielsen equivalence, the product replacement algorithm, and the finite analogue of the Andrews–Curtis moves

## 1. Scope, and the one paragraph summary

A balanced presentation of the trivial group is an `n`-tuple of words in `F_n` whose normal closure is all of `F_n`. The Andrews–Curtis moves act on such tuples by inversion of one entry, by multiplication of one entry by another, and by conjugating one entry by an arbitrary word in the generators. Borovik, Lubotzky and Myasnikov put the same three moves into an arbitrary group `G`, where they act on the normally generating `k`-tuples, and they computed the connected components of the resulting graph for every finite `G` [BLM 2005]. That result is the finite counterpart of the conjecture, and this file is mostly about it.

The summary is this. For finite `G` the components of the Andrews–Curtis graph are exactly the preimages of the components of the graph of the abelianisation, and the abelian components are governed by a determinant modulo a torsion modulus [BLM 2005, Fact 1.2]. Because the free group has no torsion and every generating `n`-tuple of `Z^n` is a basis, that reduction carries no information in the free case, and the free case of the same reduction statement is the conjecture itself. The finite analogue therefore proves a real theorem and explains, sharply, why the theorem cannot be transported.

## 2. The four move sets, precisely

### 2.1 Elementary Nielsen transformations and Nielsen equivalence

Let `F_k` be free on `x_1, ..., x_k` and let `G` be a group. The elementary Nielsen transformations of `G^k` are the following three families of moves on a `k`-tuple `(x_1, ..., x_k)` [BLM 2005, Section 1], quoted:

* (1) `(x_1, ..., x_i, ..., x_k) -> (x_1, ..., x_i x_j^{+-1}, ..., x_k)`, for `i` different from `j`;
* (2) `(x_1, ..., x_i, ..., x_k) -> (x_1, ..., x_j^{+-1} x_i, ..., x_k)`, for `i` different from `j`;
* (3) `(x_1, ..., x_i, ..., x_k) -> (x_1, ..., x_i^{-1}, ..., x_k)`.

For `G = F_k` these moves are induced by automorphisms of `F_k`, and Nielsen's theorem states that the elementary Nielsen transformations generate `Aut(F_k)` [Nielsen 1924]. Two generating `k`-tuples of a group `K` are **Nielsen equivalent** when one is carried to the other by a sequence of these moves, which is the same as lying in one orbit of `Aut(F_k)` acting on `Epi(F_k, K)` by precomposition [MW 2013, Section 1]. Nielsen equivalence classes are also called Nielsen classes, and [MW 2013] writes `N` for the set of them.

Two conventions are in the literature, and they are not the same. Nielsen classes are the orbits of `Aut(F_k)` alone. Dunwoody's `T_k`-systems are the orbits of the group generated by the images of `Aut(F_n)` **and** of `Aut(G)` [Dunwoody 1963, Section 1]. The second convention is coarser, and Section 6 gives a case where the two counts differ by a factor of two (the Heisenberg group of order 125).

### 2.2 Andrews–Curtis moves and the Andrews–Curtis graph

Borovik, Lubotzky and Myasnikov add a fourth family to the list above, quoted:

* (4) `(x_1, ..., x_i, ..., x_k) -> (x_1, ..., x_i^{w}, ..., x_k)`, with `w` in `S` union `S^{-1}`, a subset of `G`,

where `x^w` denotes conjugation and `S` is a fixed subset of `G`. Moves (1) to (4) are the `AC_S`-moves, and with `S = G` they are the `AC`-moves [BLM 2005, Section 1]. Their key observation is that the elementary Nielsen moves carry generating tuples to generating tuples, while the `AC`-moves carry **normally** generating tuples to normally generating tuples, because conjugation does not change a normal closure [BLM 2005, Section 1].

Write `N_k(G)` for the normally generating `k`-tuples of `G`. The Andrews–Curtis graph `Δ_k(G)` has vertex set `N_k(G)`, and two vertices are adjacent when one is obtained from the other by an elementary `AC`-move [BLM 2005, Section 1]. In the free case the vertices of `Δ_k(F_k)` are exactly the `k`-generator `k`-relator presentations of the trivial group, and the standard tuple `(x_1, ..., x_k)` is a vertex. The conjecture then reads as follows, quoted from [BLM 2005]:

> AC-Conjecture: For a free group `F_k` of rank `k >= 2`, the Andrews–Curtis graph `Δ_k(F_k)` is connected.

This is the formulation used in the rest of the file. Two remarks on the move set are worth recording. First, the single-component conjugation move is what separates the Andrews–Curtis problem from Nielsen's transitivity theorem for bases of a free group, since `Aut(F_k)` is transitive on bases and `Δ_k(F_k)` is not known to be connected. Second, the conjecture is also stated in the form that every normally generating `n`-tuple of `F_n` can be reduced to a basis by Nielsen transformations and arbitrary conjugations, and that is the phrasing of the abstract of [Guyot 2018]. The concrete move list used in this file is (1) to (4), which is the list of [BLM 2005].

### 2.3 The product replacement graph

The product replacement algorithm runs a random walk on generating `k`-tuples of a finite group `G`. The moves are, quoted from [LP 2001, Section 1]:

* `R^{+-}_{i,j} : (g_1, ..., g_i, ..., g_k) -> (g_1, ..., g_i g_j^{+-1}, ..., g_k)`,
* `L^{+-}_{i,j} : (g_1, ..., g_i, ..., g_k) -> (g_1, ..., g_j^{+-1} g_i, ..., g_k)`.

The graph `Γ_k(G)` on generating tuples with these edges is `4k(k-1)`-regular, possibly with loops [LP 2001, Section 1]. It differs from the abelian case of `Δ_k(G)` in two ways, quoted from [BLM 2005]: the vertices of `Γ_k(A)` are the same as those of `Δ_k(A)`, and "the only difference between `Γ_k(A)` and `Δ_k(A)` is that the former has edges defined only by 'transvections' (1)–(2), while in the latter the inversion of components (3) is also allowed."

### 2.4 A dictionary between the free case and the finite case

| free group `F_k` | finite group `G` |
| --- | --- |
| `k`-tuple of words, generating a normal subgroup | `k`-tuple in `N_k(G)`, generating `G` normally |
| inversion of one entry | `x_i` maps to `x_i^{-1}` |
| multiplication of one entry by another | `x_i` maps to `x_i x_j^{+-1}` and to `x_j^{+-1} x_i` |
| conjugation of one entry by any word in `F_k` | conjugation of one entry by any element of `G` |
| the graph is infinite, and every vertex has infinitely many neighbours | the graph is finite, and each vertex has at most `4k(k-1) + k + k` times `ord(G)` neighbours |
| the conjecture says the graph is connected | the components are described by [BLM 2005, Theorem 1.1] |

The last row of the table is the structural fact that governs Section 7. A finite group gives a finite regular graph, so expansion, mixing time and diameter are meaningful there. In `F_k` the conjugation move alone produces infinitely many neighbours, so none of those tools has a direct analogue.

## 3. What the finite analogue proves

### 3.1 The main theorem, and its corollary

The central result is quoted here in full because everything else in this file depends on it [BLM 2005, Theorem 1.1]:

> Let `G` be a finite group and `k >= max{d_G(G), 2}`. Then two tuples `U, V` from `N_k(G)` are AC-equivalent if and only if they are AC-equivalent in the abelianisation `Ab(G) = G/[G,G]`, i.e., the connected components of the AC-graph `Δ_k(G)` are precisely the preimages of the connected components of the AC-graph `Δ_k(Ab(G))`.

Here `d_G(G)` is the minimal number of normal generators of `G` [BLM 2005, Section 1]. The corollary that matters for the conjecture is also quoted [BLM 2005, Corollary 1.3]:

> For any `k >= 2`, and any epimorphism `φ : F_k -> G` onto a finite group `G`, the image of `Δ_k(F_k)` in `Δ_k(G)` is connected.

The context of that corollary is a question raised in [BLM 2005, reference 6]: one might hope to refute the conjecture by finding a finite group `G` and a homomorphism `φ : F_2 -> G` for which the images of the Akbulut–Kirby pair `(u, v_n) = (x y x y^{-1} x^{-1} y^{-1}, x^n y^{-(n+1)})` and of `(x, y)` fall in different components of `Δ_2(G)`. The corollary rules that out, and the authors say so: their result "leaves no hope of finding an counterexample to the Andrews Curtis conjecture by looking at the connected components of the Andrews Curtis graphs of finite groups" [BLM 2005].

### 3.2 The abelian case, where the components are counted

For a finite abelian group `A = Z_1 x ... x Z_d` with `|Z_i|` dividing `|Z_j|` for `i` smaller than `j`, the components of `Δ_k(A)` are described as follows, quoted from [BLM 2005, Fact 1.2], which the authors attribute to Diaconis and Graham after "a slight modification of their proof":

* (a) if `k > d` then `Δ_k(A)` is connected;
* (b) if `k = d >= 2`, let `m = |Z_1|`; then `Δ_d(A)` has `φ(m)/2` connected components, each represented by a tuple `(z_1^λ, z_2, ..., z_d)` with `λ` in the unit group modulo `m`, and two such tuples lie in the same component exactly when `λ = ±μ`.

The underlying theorem for the transvection graph alone is [DG 1999, Theorem], quoted:

> The graph of ordered generating `t`-tuples is connected for `t >= n+1`. For `t = n` the graph has `φ(m_n)` components. If `g_1, ..., g_n` are written as `g_i = (a_{i1}, ..., a_{in})`, the components have constant values of `det(a_{ij})` (mod `m_n`).

Here `m_{i+1}` divides `m_i`, so `m_n` is the smallest invariant factor and also the exponent of `A`. The two statements are consistent, since `λ` mapping to `-λ` is exactly the effect of inverting one coordinate on the determinant label, so passing from the transvection graph to `Δ` identifies the labels `λ` and `-λ`. The printed formula `φ(m)/2` needs the obvious reading when `m = 2`, because there is a single unit and `λ = -λ`; Section 6 reports the computed counts, which are `1` in that case and `φ(m)/2` for `m >= 3`.

This is the precise sense in which a finite abelian group carries an obstruction that `Z^n` does not: the obstruction is a determinant taken modulo a torsion modulus, and `Z^n` has none.

### 3.3 The generalised problem, and why its free case is the conjecture

The authors generalise their theorem into a definition, quoted [BLM 2005]:

> We say that a group `G` satisfies the generalised Andrews Curtis conjecture if for any `k >= max{d_G(G), 2}` tuples `U, V` in `N_k(G)` are AC-equivalent in `G` if and only if their images are AC-equivalent in the abelianisation `Ab(G)`.

They then pose the problem of finding a group `G` which does not satisfy the generalised conjecture, and they suggest looking at the Grigorchuk group [BLM 2005]. Part of that problem is settled: every finitely generated soluble group satisfies the generalised conjecture [Guyot 2018, Abstract], so a counterexample has to be non-soluble. The following observation is an argument, and it is short.

**Argument.** Take `G = F_k`. Its abelianisation is `Z^k`, and every generating `k`-tuple of `Z^k` is a `Z`-basis: an integer `k x k` matrix whose columns generate `Z^k` is surjective, hence has determinant `+-1` by the Smith normal form. The elementary transvections and the sign changes generate `GL_k(Z)`, so `Δ_k(Z^k)` is connected. Therefore the condition that the images are AC-equivalent in the abelianisation is vacuous for `G = F_k`, and the generalised conjecture for `F_k` says exactly that all normally generating `k`-tuples of `F_k` are AC-equivalent, which is the classical conjecture. Any proof of the generalised conjecture for all groups would therefore prove the Andrews–Curtis conjecture, and no argument that establishes the generalised statement for `F_k` can be easier than the conjecture itself.

### 3.4 Why finite quotients cannot help, in one paragraph

The Andrews–Curtis moves are Tietze transformations of a restricted kind, so they preserve the group presented by the tuple. Two balanced presentations of the trivial group present the same group, so every functor of the presented group, and in particular every finite quotient, gives the same value on both. The argument does not depend on which invariant of finite quotients is chosen, and it is the reason the survey of invariants in [invariants.md](invariants.md) works with invariants of the tuple itself. The result of [BLM 2005] strengthens this for the graph-theoretic strategy, since it shows that even the finest finite-group analysis of the tuple map cannot separate two tuples that both present the trivial group.

## 4. Nielsen classes inside finite groups

### 4.1 Wiegold's conjecture

The question that replaces the Andrews–Curtis conjecture in the Nielsen setting is the uniqueness of Nielsen classes, and its standard form for finite simple groups is quoted from [KW 2010]:

> The Wiegold conjecture says that if `G` is a finite simple group and `k >= 3` then any two generating `k`-tuples of `G` are Nielsen equivalent; in other words the conjecture says that the action of `Aut(F_k)` on `Epi(F_k, G)` is transitive in this case.

The same convention is used in [MW 2013]. Dunwoody's `T_k`-systems use a slightly different equivalence, since automorphisms of `G` are allowed as well [Dunwoody 1963]. For finite simple groups the two versions have the same flavour, and Section 6 reports both counts for the groups I tested.

### 4.2 What is known in the positive direction

* For a finitely generated abelian group the action of `Aut(F_k)` on `Epi(F_k, G)` is transitive when `k >= rank(G) + 1`, and for `k = rank(G)` the number of Nielsen classes is finite and is computed from the primary decomposition; this is reported in [KW 2010, Section 1] with references to the Neumanns and to Diaconis and Graham, and the transvection graph count is the theorem of [DG 1999] quoted above.
* For `PSL(2,p)` with `p >= 5` and `k >= 3` there is a single `T_k`-system. This is attributed to Gilman [Gilman 1977] in [KW 2010] and in [Ree 2025], and Section 6 checks the case `A_5` isomorphic to `PSL(2,5)` at `k = 3` by exhaustive computation.
* Further finite simple groups are treated by Evans [Evans 1993], whose title states the subject.
* For the small Ree groups `2G_2(3^{2e+1})` the conjecture is announced for all `k >= 5` in [Ree 2025].

### 4.3 What is known in the negative direction

Uniqueness fails in general when `k` equals the minimal number of generators. The classical family is due to Dunwoody [Dunwoody 1963, Theorem 1], quoted:

> To every pair of integers `n > 1` and `N > 0` and every prime `p`, there exists a `p`-group which is nilpotent of class 2 and has at least `N` T-systems of generating `n`-vectors.

Dunwoody also records that an abelian group generated by `n` elements has one `T`-system of generating `n`-vectors, and that B. H. Neumann had constructed a finite 2-group of nilpotency class 10 and soluble length 3 with at least two `T`-systems of generating 2-vectors, answering a question of Gaschütz [Dunwoody 1963, Section 1]. For `A_5` at `k = 2` my computation in Section 6 finds three `Aut(F_2)`-classes and two `T_2`-systems, so non-uniqueness occurs already in the smallest nonabelian simple group, and the two conventions give visibly different numbers.

### 4.4 Nielsen classes of generating pairs: the finite-group programme

For generating pairs there is a developed theory, and [MW 2013] is the cleanest entry point. Their definitions are quoted:

> two generating pairs are called Nielsen equivalent if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of operations of replacing one generator by one of its two products with the other or with the inverse of the other.

They record that the union of the conjugacy classes of the commutator of a generating pair and of its inverse is an invariant of the Nielsen class, the Higman invariant, and that for `SL(2,q)` and `PSL(2,q)` the trace of the commutator gives an invariant in the field [MW 2013, Section 1]. Their classification conjecture says that the Higman invariant is a complete invariant for `SL(2,q)`, and they verify it computationally for all `q <= 101` [MW 2013, Abstract]. This is the model of a complete answer for a family of finite groups, and it is the closest thing in the finite world to the statement the Andrews–Curtis conjecture wants for free groups.

The Higman invariant is a Nielsen invariant, and Nielsen equivalence is finer than the Andrews–Curtis equivalence, so it cannot be used directly against the conjecture. Section 7.3 explains why it collapses under the conjugation move.

## 5. The product replacement algorithm: what was proved about these graphs

The algorithm outputs a random component of a random walk on `Γ_k(G)` for `t` steps [LP 2001, Section 1]. The firm results about the graphs are the following.

* **Connectivity.** For `k >= 2 log_2|G|` the graph `Γ_k(G)` is connected, which is reported as the result of the original paper [Celler et al. 1995] in [Pak 2000]. Pak also reports that the diameter is `O(k log|G|)` in that range, and attributes that to Babai.
* **Components in the abelian case.** For `k` equal to the number of invariant factors the graph has `φ(m_n)` components, labelled by the determinant modulo `m_n` [DG 1999, Theorem]. The identification of `λ` with `-λ` then gives Fact 1.2 of [BLM 2005] for the graph with inversions.
* **Mixing time.** For `k = Ω(log|G| log log|G|)` the lazy walk on `Γ_k(G)` has mixing time polynomial in `log|G|` [Pak 2000, Main Theorem]. For abelian `G` the mixing time is at most `C k^5 log|G|`, and for nilpotent `G` of class at most `i` it is at most `C(k,i) log|G|`, under the hypothesis that the relevant special automorphism group has Kazhdan's property (T) [LP 2001, Theorems 1, 2 and 3].

None of these statements concerns `F_k`, and the reason is structural. The graphs in the list are finite and regular because `G` is finite, while `Δ_k(F_k)` has infinitely many neighbours at every vertex on account of the conjugation move. A random walk or a diameter bound therefore has no direct counterpart in the free case, and the only quantity that survives is the length of a path between two named vertices, which is the subject of Bridson's lower bounds and of [complexity.md](complexity.md).

## 6. An exact census of small groups

### 6.1 What the script does

[nielsen_census.py](nielsen_census.py) builds a finite group as a full multiplication table, computes normal closures through conjugacy classes, and then runs an exhaustive breadth first search on the relevant graphs, with the following choices.

* Vertices are either the normally generating `k`-tuples (the convention of [BLM 2005]) or the generating `k`-tuples (the convention of Nielsen classes and of `Γ_k(G)`), and both are computed.
* The moves are exactly (1) to (4) of Section 2, with `S = G` and also, as a check, with `S` a generating set of size two or three.
* Coordinate permutations are available as an extra option, because they are Nielsen transformations, and with them the classes of generating tuples are exactly the `Aut(F_k)`-orbits in the sense of [MW 2013]. In every group tested the permutation moves changed no class, so the two counts coincide in the table.
* Automorphisms of `G` are computed by brute force from a generating set, and used to merge Nielsen classes into Dunwoody's `T_k`-systems.
* The abelianisation is computed as a quotient table, so the reduction of [BLM 2005, Theorem 1.1] can be checked without external data.

The command is

```
python3 survey/nielsen_census.py
```

It takes about 37 seconds on the 4-CPU virtual machine of this run, uses only the Python standard library, has no random choices, and writes `nielsen_census.out.txt` and `nielsen_census.json` next to itself. The SHA-256 of the printed report of the run copied into this directory is

```

```

The digest is computed over the report text that precedes the digest line in the printed run, it is printed as the last line of the run, and it is not stored in the two output files.

### 6.3 The published statements that the run checks

Each of the following was checked by the script, and the string `PASS` appears in the output for each instance.

1. **The group axioms** for every table, by exhaustive associativity and inverse checks.
2. **Moves preserve the vertex set.** Every `AC`-move carries a normally generating tuple to a normally generating tuple, checked exhaustively for all groups of order at most 12; every Nielsen move carries a generating tuple to a generating tuple, checked the same way.
3. **[BLM 2005, Theorem 1.1]**, that the components of `Δ_k(G)` are the preimages of the components of `Δ_k(Ab G)`, checked on 21 group and rank pairs. The one non-vacuous instance is `Heis_5` at `k = 2`: `Δ_2(Heis_5)` has exactly two components, of 6000 vertices each, and `Δ_2(C_5 x C_5)` has exactly two components, of 240 vertices each.
4. **[BLM 2005, Corollary 1.3] in the concrete form used against the Akbulut–Kirby family.** For every normally generating pair `(a,b)` of each of the nine groups tested, the pair `(u,v)` with `u = a b a (b a b)^{-1}` and `v = a^3 b^{-4}` is again normally generating and lies in the same component of `Δ_2(G)` as `(a,b)`. The counts are `3599` pairs for `A_5`, `10800` for `S_5`, and smaller numbers for the rest, with zero failures.
5. **The generating set reduction.** Conjugation by the elements of a two or three element generating set produces the same partition of the vertices as conjugation by all of `G`, in every case tested. This is consistent with the remark in [BLM 2005] that connectivity of `Δ^S_k(G)` and of `Δ_k(G)` agree when `S` generates `G`.
6. **[DG 1999, Theorem]** for the transvection graph, with the counts `φ(m_n)`, on nine abelian groups.
7. **Uniqueness at `k = 3` for `A_5`.** The number of Nielsen classes of generating triples is `1`, and the number of `T_3`-systems is `1`, consistent with the attribution of the `PSL(2,p)`, `p >= 5`, `k >= 3` case to [Gilman 1977].
8. **Non-uniqueness at `k = 2` for `A_5`**, with three `Aut(F_2)`-classes of generating pairs of sizes 1080, 600 and 600, and two classes modulo `Aut(A_5)`.
9. **The conjugation move can destroy ordinary generation**, which is why the vertices have to be normally generating tuples. In `A_5` the pair `(a, b) = ((345), (123))` generates `A_5`, while conjugating the first entry by `w = (243)` gives `(w a w^{-1}, b) = ((235), (123))`, which generates a subgroup of order 12. The script finds the first such example in each permutation group and counts the failures; in `Heis_5` there is no example, since every single-entry conjugation of a generating pair of that group still generates it.

The count with inversions matches the number of `±`-classes in all nine cases, and that number equals `φ(m)/2` for `m >= 3` and `1` for `m = 2`. The four cases with `m = 2` are the ones where the printed expression `φ(m)/2` is not an integer, and the computation shows that the value is `1` there. Apart from that boundary, the printed formula is confirmed.

### 6.5 What the numbers show, and what they do not show

Four observations follow from the table.

The conjugation move is very strong in the finite setting. For `A_5` at `k = 2` the Nielsen graph on normally generating pairs has 66 components, while the Andrews–Curtis graph has one, and for `A_5` at `k = 3` the corresponding numbers are 58 and one. Any invariant of the tuple that is not invariant under conjugation by arbitrary elements of `G` is therefore destroyed in the finite world, which is what allows a clean theorem there.

Conjugation is not always strong enough. For `Heis_5` at `k = 2` the Andrews–Curtis graph has two components, so the two Nielsen classes survive, and they are separated exactly by the determinant modulo 5 that [DG 1999] uses. This is the smallest illustration of the mechanism the conjecture would have to defeat in the free case.

The conjugation move is not simply a change of basis of the tuple. In `A_5` the pair `((345), (123))` generates the whole group, while conjugating the first entry by `(243)` leaves a pair that generates a subgroup of order 12, so ordinary generation is destroyed 28080 times among the generating pairs and conjugating elements of `A_5`, and 168480 times in `S_5`. The conjecture lives on normally generating tuples precisely because the normal closure is what the conjugation move leaves fixed.

Automorphisms of `G` can be stronger than the conjugation moves. For `Heis_5` the two components of `Δ_2(G)` merge when automorphisms of `G` are allowed, so the `T_2`-system count is one while the Andrews–Curtis component count is two. The relation between the two equivalence relations is therefore not a matter of convention, and results stated for `T_k`-systems do not transfer automatically to `Δ_k(G)`.

Nothing in the table bears on the conjecture. All the groups have at most 125 elements, the largest graph has 215999 vertices, and every statement checked is about finite groups. The census tests the finite theory against itself and against the published theorems, and it cannot produce evidence for or against the connectivity of `Δ_k(F_k)`.

## 7. What transfers to the Andrews–Curtis conjecture, and what does not

### 7.1 Bookkeeping that transfers

The graph formulation transfers without change, and it is what makes the finite computation comparable with the free problem. Three pieces of bookkeeping were checked in Section 6 and can be reused in the free case.

* Vertices must be normally generating tuples, because the conjecture is about presentations of the trivial group and because the conjugation move preserves normal generation while it can destroy ordinary generation. The verified example is in Section 6.3: in `A_5` the pair `((345), (123))` generates, and conjugating the first entry by `(243)` gives a pair that generates a subgroup of order 12.
* The conjugation move may be restricted to a generating set of the ambient group. In the free case the ambient group is `F_k`, and conjugating by the generators alone is strictly weaker than what the free group allows, so the check in Section 6.3 point 5 marks the limit of that transfer.
* Nielsen classes refine Andrews–Curtis classes, so any Nielsen invariant is a candidate obstruction only after it is shown to be constant on Andrews–Curtis classes. This is the filter that Section 7.3 applies to the Higman invariant.

### 7.2 The reduction to the abelianisation cannot transfer

This is the sharpest point in the file, and it is the argument of Section 3.3. Theorem 1.1 of [BLM 2005] reduces the finite case to a finite abelian group, where a determinant modulo a torsion modulus produces the components. In the free case the same reduction would compare `Δ_k(F_k)` with `Δ_k(Z^k)`, and the second graph is connected because `Z^k` has no torsion and every generating `k`-tuple is a basis. The generalised statement for `F_k` is therefore equivalent to the conjecture, so the finite proof cannot be adapted without proving the conjecture. The obstruction that the finite theory uses is a torsion phenomenon, and torsion is exactly what a free group does not have.

The same argument explains why no finite quotient can help, which is the point of Section 3.4 and of [invariants.md](invariants.md). The torsion of `G` is what makes the finite theory work, and a balanced presentation of the trivial group has no nontrivial finite quotient to see.

### 7.3 Conjugacy-class invariants die under the conjugation move

The Higman invariant of a generating pair is the union of the conjugacy classes of `[a,b]` and of its inverse [MW 2013, Section 1]. It is preserved by Nielsen moves. It is not preserved by the conjugation move of the Andrews–Curtis list, and the computation is one line:

`[w a w^{-1}, b] = w a w^{-1} b w a^{-1} w^{-1} b^{-1} = w [a, w^{-1} b w] w^{-1}`.

So conjugating one entry replaces the commutator by a conjugate of the commutator with a conjugate of the other entry, and the conjugacy class of `[a,b]` can change. Since the Andrews–Curtis moves include this operation, the Higman invariant and the trace invariants of [MW 2013] do not descend to Andrews–Curtis classes. They are obstructions to Nielsen equivalence, and Nielsen equivalence is strictly finer, so no obstruction of this type can separate two tuples that are Nielsen inequivalent yet Andrews–Curtis equivalent. The finite computation shows the same phenomenon in numbers: 66 Nielsen classes and one Andrews–Curtis component for `A_5` at `k = 2`.

### 7.4 Algorithmic tools do not transfer, and path lengths do

The product replacement toolbox measures the connectivity and the mixing time of a finite regular graph [Celler et al. 1995, DG 1999, Pak 2000, LP 2001]. In the free case the graph has infinite degree, and the only comparable quantity is the length of a path between two given tuples. That quantity has lower bounds due to Bridson, which are the subject of [complexity.md](complexity.md), and it has heuristic upper bounds from search, which belong to the computational part of this round (direction D4, whose survey was not yet in place when this file was finalised). The finite results are therefore useful as a supply of test cases and of structural predictions, and they do not supply proofs.

### 7.5 Rank two: one-sided Nielsen transformations

There is one place where the Nielsen theory gives a complete structural result about a graph on pairs in a free group, and it is due to Vernitski [Vernitski 2009]. The graph has pairs of elements of a free group as vertices, with edges `(u,v) -> (vu,v)`, `(u,v) -> (v^{-1}u,v)`, `(u,v) -> (u,uv)` and `(u,v) -> (u,u^{-1}v)`, and the paper describes the connected components and the shortest pair in every component, and derives a linear time algorithm for the component problem [Vernitski 2009, Abstract]. The moves are one-sided Nielsen transformations, so the graph is a subgraph of the Nielsen graph and its components refine the Nielsen classes. This is a positive result about a rank two graph that is close to, though weaker than, the Andrews–Curtis graph, and it is the kind of statement one would want for `Δ_2(F_2)`. Whether the component description can be extended to the conjugations of the Andrews–Curtis list is not known to me, and Section 8 records it as a question.

### 7.6 The dimensional analogy

Lustig's paper opens with the analogy that organises this whole area, quoted from the abstract [Lustig 1991]:

> Nielsen equivalence and simple-homotopy equivalence are interpreted as analogues in dimensions 1 and 2 respectively.

The Andrews–Curtis moves correspond to a restricted class of simple homotopy equivalences between two-dimensional complexes, which is the content of the topological side of the problem and is treated in [topology.md](topology.md). The Nielsen results of Section 4 are therefore the one-dimensional model, and the fact that the one-dimensional model is settled while the two-dimensional one is not is the reason the analogy is worth stating.

## 8. Questions this file leaves open

These are stated precisely and rated in the style of [OPEN_QUESTIONS.md](../OPEN_QUESTIONS.md). Each is a question about the material above, and none is a claim.

1. **Is Wiegold's conjecture true for every finite simple group at `k = 3`?** It is known for `PSL(2,p)` and for several other families, and it is open in general. A counterexample would be a finite simple group with two Nielsen classes of generating triples, and it would say something about how much the conjugation moves do in the finite world.
2. **Is there a finitely generated infinite group for which the generalised Andrews–Curtis conjecture of [BLM 2005] fails?** The authors pose exactly this problem and suggest the Grigorchuk group, and [Guyot 2018] rules out the soluble case. Such a group would not refute the conjecture, and it would show where the finiteness hypothesis of Theorem 1.1 is needed.
3. **Can the component description of [Vernitski 2009] for one-sided Nielsen transformations be extended to the conjugation moves of the Andrews–Curtis list at rank two?** A positive answer would decide the rank two case, and a negative answer at a specific pair would be a counterexample to the conjecture, so the question is as hard as the rank two case in one direction.
4. **Is there an algorithm that decides Andrews–Curtis equivalence of two balanced tuples at rank two?** The finite computation in Section 6 can decide the analogous question in a finite group by exhaustion, and nothing similar is known in `F_2`.
5. **Which invariants of a tuple survive the conjugation move, and are computable?** The Higman and trace invariants fail Section 7.3, and the torsion invariants of Section 7.2 have no analogue in `Z^k`. A complete answer would identify the exact boundary of the Nielsen method.
6. **Does the number of Nielsen classes of generating pairs of a finite simple group behave regularly in the rank?** The computed values here are three for `A_5` and three for `S_5` at `k = 2`, and one for `A_5` at `k = 3`. A formula or a monotonicity statement is not known to me.
